The detail

Measurements, screens, and my limits

Working behind the three front-page figures, nothing new.

Three other companies bought this software under signed contracts, with me named as supplier. My mother introduced them and set each company up to watch its own books before deciding. Not strangers who found me.

The evidence is narrower: a working chief accountant put her professional name behind it, and three businesses signed and paid after seeing it run on their own data. Not an arm’s-length decision by people with no connection to me.

Not a side business competing with a job: I do not sell it, seek no more customers, and turned down the work they would bring. I would say so on day one.

My mother, a chief accountant

Her practice in Vietnam keeps the books for 43 client businesses. Every sale, purchase and bank line is recorded and checked; totals go to the tax office on a return.

One number wrong and a real business gets the wrong tax bill; in Vietnam the chief accountant signs the accounts by name and answers for them.

So I built software to do the reading. It went live on 29 May 2026, the day after I started. Everything since was written while the practice relied on it: no safe empty version to get wrong. She corrected me throughout; she is the qualified accountant, I am not.

The typing

Somebody typed all of this

Every invoice must reach the books: who from, what for, how much, how much of that was tax. Of 42,655 invoices here, 93 were typed by hand.

The other 42,562 arrived as files: 32,158 electronic invoices from the supplier, 10,404 from its online list.

The measurements

Almost all typing, little deciding

Three totals, not meant to add up: 70,320 journal entries from 42,655 invoices, 1.65 each; a bank statement line is a third thing.

Invoices that arrived as a file, so nobody typed them99.8%

42,562 of 42,655 invoices. About 1 in 459 typed by hand: marked manual, no import record.

Entries where the software worked out which account to use32.3%

22,680 of 70,320 journal entries, about 1 in 3. A journal entry is one transaction: two or more lines with a date, an amount, and an account on each side. Choosing which account is the judgement. A person approves every one; the log names that person and credits the system with the writing.

Bank lines matched to one particular invoice5.2%

1,239 of 23,671 statement lines. A statement line is one row on a bank statement: money in or out, plus the text the bank printed. Matching one to the invoice it paid is the job; only about 1 in 19 can be. Expected: the other 22,432 are wages, tax, transfers and fees.

Counted on the working system on 5 September 2026.

What one press does

Saving invoices: 251 presses took in 4,413 invoices, 17.6 each; the biggest press took 104 invoices, finished inside the same second. That 251 is not every press: it covers only the period since the log recorded which rows came from which press, 4,484 of the 32,356 saving actions.

When it reads the invoices correctly I stop worrying about the company name, the tax number, the goods amount, the tax amount. Receivables and payables come out right and are easy to reconcile. The time saved on typing means I can take on more companies, and get to the other work.

On what she still checks by hand

Every company posts things differently, so I still open each invoice and read through them once. If they are right, posting the batch is very quick. The bank statement is the same. I read the payment description to see whether the software picked the right party. Then I am at ease, and I only do it once. If I posted the whole batch on its suggestion and one was wrong, redoing it would cost a lot of time.

In mid June about 4.5 hours a week went into each company. Weekly hours for everyone went from 8 in the first week of June to 71 in the busiest week of August. The two lines count different people and do not add up: per-company is the three regular users, the 8-to-71 is all nine. 42 companies at under an hour a week is about 29 hours, not 71. (The 43 quoted elsewhere is today’s count, four days later.)

The same three did the regular work throughout: extra companies without hiring. A company takes most of its hours in its first weeks, when its old books are loaded in, so a settled company is cheaper than a new one.

A gap I would fix next

Invoices can be saved one at a time or in a batch. The log shows the batch way on 22 of 7,967 posting presses; everything else went one at a time, though one batch press once wrote 101 journal entries. The 101 counts entries, the 104 above counts invoices.

Nobody did anything wrong; the faster way was not the habit. That is the work I want my next job to be about.

What these numbers do not show.

Nothing to compare against. Nobody measured the years before the software: that work was in spreadsheets, and no spreadsheet records how long it took. So no hours saved figure here; any I gave would be invented.

The work got slower on paper, not faster. Seconds of recorded activity per journal entry: 26.5 in June, 23.9 in July, 33.0 in August, 37.4 in September. Recorded hours at the keyboard: 105, 181 and 236 over the first three full months, across 23, 31 and 31 days worked: 4.6 hours a day rising to 7.6.

The root cause, as far as I can tell. The books got bigger, not easier: more companies keep coming, and 70% of entries belong to periods closed before go live, mostly old books loaded in. I cannot prove from this data that each task got quicker, so I do not say it.

One of these is a rule, not a measurement. Entries whose two sides disagree are refused at saving, enforced in code and covered by tests.

The other half

Two documents behind the software

Software came out; these went in, and they are the work I am applying to do. The first was written before anything was built. The second came nine weeks after the software went live, when “she has been using it” stopped answering “does it work”. It is the script itself, not a completed run: the accountant is the one who would run it and sign the result.

A business rule, specified is the input: legal basis, trigger, decision table, edge cases, and the version I got wrong. The user acceptance test (UAT) script is the output: 32 steps with measurable criteria; a failure in the first two groups stops the release.

Every number, with its query is the third: the SQL behind each figure here.

Where the VAT return comes from is the fourth and closest to the job: one statutory return mapped end to end, the ledger source of every box, the rule applied on the way, the control proving the two agree, and the four places where the software deliberately refuses to fill a box.

The first two are translated from the Vietnamese working documents, untidied.

My part

Mine and not mine

Mine, end to end: requirements; accounting and tax rules kept current as the law changes; what the software may decide alone and what a person must confirm; test strategy; releases, backups and rollback.

The decision I am most sure was right is one where I shipped nothing: the bank statement automation on the main page, wrong in 18 of its 2,466 suggestions, recorded in the project as rejected for now, not as a failure.

The subject expert was the chief accountant at the next desk. I turned what she knew into rules a system could follow, then checked every answer with her.

Graduate Business Analyst adverts in Auckland usually ask for the same things: gathering requirements, workshops with the people affected, mapping how work flows, writing it down, helping test before go live. That is the list this project ran on.

AI wrote most of it

AI coding tools wrote most of the implementation, under my direction.

An AI cannot decide which tax rule applies this month, or carry responsibility when a number is wrong. That part was mine.

The finished software calls no AI model to do arithmetic or choose an account. Written rules make those choices, and a test fails the build if anyone adds such a call.

I am not claiming AI made me faster: I never measured my own speed. The best known trial, by the research group METR in 2025, was marked out of date by its own authors, whose 2026 follow up calls the evidence weak.

What the use shows

Used on 94 of the 100 days since it went live. Of thirteen logins one is the system’s own machine account; nine people have actually worked in it, eight at the firm and me. The chief accountant alone is 55.9% of 409,668 recorded actions.

That shows the practice depends on it, not that every number is right: the rule I got wrong proves that.
